I'm Mark Breitenbach. Most people who've known me for a while call me Geo. I live in Colorado and run a small game studio with two partners, where the engineering team is mostly agents and I'm the one managing it. I have a computer science degree and I have never held a job writing code. Thirty years of systems, architecture and management got me here instead, and this site is where I write down what running an agent team looks like from that chair. When a tool is cheaper to build than to rent, I build it; the studio's ticketing system is the current example.

// how I got here

The short version is that I have specialized, been displaced, and retooled more times than I can comfortably count. The long version, in order:

  1. Test engineer at Citrix. Breaking other people's software, methodically, and writing down how.
  2. Systems administrator for a run of internet companies. The other side of the same coin: keeping systems up instead of finding where they fall over. Scripting became the job within the job.
  3. Systems engineer at Hunter Douglas. Designing systems rather than tending them.
  4. Technical educator at Sun Microsystems, for several years. The same material, now explained to a room instead of applied to a machine. I'm a technician who learned to teach, not a teacher who learned the tech, and it's the work I've liked most.
  5. Data center architect, still at Sun. The high-end classes needed hardware too expensive to put in every teaching center, so someone had to design the remote lab that served them all. Teaching's problem, solved with architecture.
  6. Technical duty officer at eBay, running the overnight network operations center. The first management role and the largest shift: not fixing the outage, but directing the people who could, under a clock, with enough of the troubleshooting in hand to direct them well.
  7. Systems engineer at Intrado. Back to building systems, after years of running and teaching them.
  8. Government contracting: agile coaching, project management, and development team management. The engineering moved to other people's hands; the work became process, planning, and the people doing it.
  9. Now: a game studio, an engineering team made of agents, and the decision to learn this properly rather than coast. Every previous reinvention replaced the skill. This one is the first that moves toward what I was always doing: deciding what to build, how to structure it so a team can work on it, what "done" means, and when to overrule the default.
